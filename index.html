From Big Time Series Forecasting to Foundation Models for Structured Data

Prediction over structured data — forecasting future values, classifying rows — has long been a central concern for the database and data-mining communities. Foundation models have now arrived for both modalities: large-scale pretraining followed by zero-shot or in-context inference, in time series (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, Toto) and on tables alike (TabPFN, TabICL, Mitra, Limix). This tutorial surveys that shift for a database audience and asks which parts of it belong inside the data system.

Scope

One line, from ARIMA to pretrained tables

Our earlier tutorials in this community — at VLDB, SIGMOD and KDD — traced the evolution from classical models and tensor methods to neural forecasting, probabilistic forecasting, and the practical systems that put them into production. This edition keeps that backbone and updates it with the most important recent development: foundation models for structured data.

It differs from the earlier versions in four ways. It expands scope from forecasting to both time-series and tabular modalities in one unified treatment. It moves the modeling narrative from deep probabilistic forecasting to pretraining, zero-shot inference, in-context learning and synthetic priors — a qualitative shift in how prediction is done. It foregrounds data-management questions the earlier tutorials did not define: schema heterogeneity, corpus curation, contamination-aware evaluation, realistic benchmarks, throughput and memory trade-offs, and governance. And it adds a systems perspective: pretrained models make it possible to embed predictive operators into analytical execution plans.

Audience

Who should attend

Database researchers, data-mining researchers, ML-systems researchers, and practitioners who want a principled introduction to modern prediction over structured data. It is especially relevant if you work on forecasting, tabular prediction, benchmarking, warehouse-native ML, AutoML, or end-to-end deployment inside data systems.

We assume familiarity with basic linear algebra, probability, and machine-learning fundamentals — supervised learning, loss functions, gradient descent — but no prior knowledge of foundation models or structured-data pretraining. You will leave with a coherent map from classical forecasting to foundation models for structured data, an understanding of the architectural choices, pretraining strategies and evaluation practices that define the current state of the art, and an awareness of the open research problems at the intersection of foundation models and data management.

Detailed outline · five modules · 90 minutes

00 – 10

From classical methods to foundation models

The historical backbone. Exponential smoothing, ARIMA, structural and Kalman-filter models; the shift from local to global neural forecasting with DeepAR, the Temporal Fusion Transformer and N-BEATS, and deep probabilistic models; tree-based tabular practice and AutoML. Sets up the paradigm shift.

10 – 40

Time-series foundation models

The design space and its evaluation: tokenization versus continuous representation, decoder-only versus encoder–decoder architectures, group attention for multivariate and covariate-informed forecasting, pretraining corpora and synthetic augmentation, zero-shot and in-context inference, and contamination-aware evaluation.

40 – 70

Tabular foundation models

Prediction on a table reframed as in-context learning. Synthetic priors and TabPFN, scaling in-context learning with TabICL and TabDPT, mixed priors in Mitra, ensembling alongside tree-based and neural baselines, and the pitfalls of tabular evaluation — contamination from web-scraped corpora, inconsistent preprocessing, unfair baselines.

70 – 85

Data management and systems implications

Case studies from the AWS forecasting stack: long-horizon capacity planning in DynamoDB, warm-pool sizing for Athena, and cache-pool provisioning in Redshift under intermittent demand. Then the forward-looking argument — expressing predictive workloads as context construction plus model invocation inside analytical query plans, and the cost modeling, plan optimization and result caching that would require.

85 – 90

Demos, open problems, and discussion

Short live demonstrations of zero-shot forecasting and in-context tabular prediction, then failure modes and open problems: multi-modal structured-data models, continual pretraining, active data acquisition, privacy-preserving pretraining, and tighter database integration. The floor is open.

Presenters

Xiyuan Zhang

Amazon Web Services

Applied Scientist at AWS, working on structured-data foundation models with an emphasis on pretraining and multimodal analysis. Lead author of Mitra and co-author of Chronos and Chronos-2. She earned her PhD in Computer Science from UC San Diego, and has co-organized the Foundation Models for Structured Data workshops at ICML 2025 and ICML 2026 and Time Series in the Age of Large Models at ICLR 2026.

Yuyang (Bernie) Wang

NTT DATA AIVista

Member of Technical Staff at NTT DATA AIVista. Previously a Principal Scientist at Amazon, where he led research in automated machine learning, time series forecasting, and foundation models. He helped build AutoGluon, GluonTS and Chronos, contributed to Amazon Forecast and SageMaker, and has delivered more than six tutorials on time series forecasting at VLDB, SIGMOD and KDD.

Abdul Fatir Ansari

Amazon Web Services

Senior Applied Scientist at AWS, focusing on time series forecasting. Lead developer of the Chronos family of time series foundation models, which ranked among the most downloaded models on Hugging Face in 2024. He received his PhD from the National University of Singapore in 2022, where he worked on deep generative models for images and time series and received the Dean’s Award for his research.

Christos Faloutsos

Carnegie Mellon University and Amazon

Professor at Carnegie Mellon University and an Amazon Scholar, holder of the Fredkin Professorship in Artificial Intelligence. An ACM Fellow, he has received the SIGKDD Innovations Award, the PAKDD Distinguished Contributions Award and 32 best-paper awards, and has published more than 500 refereed articles. His research covers large-scale data mining on graphs and time sequences, anomaly detection, tensors and fractals.

George Karypis

University of Minnesota and NTT DATA AIVista

Distinguished McKnight University Professor and William Norris Chair in Large Scale Computing at the University of Minnesota, and Chief Scientist at NTT DATA AIVista. His research spans machine learning, data mining, high-performance computing and recommender systems. He received the 2025 ACM SIGKDD Innovation Award for pioneering work in graph partitioning, recommendation systems and scalable algorithms, and is an IEEE Fellow.
